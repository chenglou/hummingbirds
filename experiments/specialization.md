# Specialization experiments

Historical experiments tested whether birds could develop useful specialties
before hatching existed. Disposable flocks used the real model, unlabeled peer
connections, and each bird's continuing conversation; they received no assigned
roles, knowledge files, or specialized tools.

## Acquired specialties

An eight-bird flock combined seeded private workshop and materials facts with
two initially empty birds. One empty bird invented two engineering designs,
retained their tradeoffs, and later recommended the better fit for a specific
constraint. The other researched an official National Park Service page with
live web search and retained its source and practical details.

Other birds discovered specialists through intermediaries, remembered their
identities and addresses, then contacted them directly for follow-up details
omitted from the original answers. This confirmed that specialists retained
more than the requesting birds had already heard.

A separate four-bird web experiment repeated this progression. The research
bird's final assistant message was only `Done.`, yet earlier search results
remained available in its continuing conversation. Public search queries did
not include the synthetic confidential project label.

These specialties emerged from experience and retained context, not prescribed
personas, external memory, or splitting.

## Discovery cost

Cold discovery reached all eight birds. Finding the private workshop specialist
took 17 completed turns; finding the bird that invented the engineering designs
took 21. Once original contributors were known, direct follow-ups each took only
three turns.

Routes prevented simple cycles but did not deduplicate converging branches: one
bird received the same request along three different paths. Negative answers,
acknowledgments, and broad forwarding added further traffic. One web-retrieval
attempt hit the experiment's turn cap; this demonstrates discovery overhead,
not absence of the requested knowledge.

## Corrections and freshness

A fresh three-bird chain first learned that a public gardening meetup was at
Maple Courtyard at 2:00 PM. Only the original source then received a correction:
Cedar Garden at 4:30 PM.

- The source did not proactively notify previously interested peers.
- Asking for the **current** schedule caused the entry bird to recheck through
  its existing intermediary and return the corrected information.
- Explicit verification with the original contributor produced a direct request,
  bypassing the intermediary.
- A later question was answered correctly from the entry bird's updated
  conversation without another peer request.

An earlier freshness probe involving a private venue and entry phrase was
inconclusive: the model refused the access-related questions instead of
consulting the source. This was safety-framing variability, not evidence that
stale information could or could not be corrected.

These results support emergent specialization, learned direct routes, and
pull-based correction without dedicated routing policy. Remaining questions
include bounded cold discovery, duplicate suppression, and whether selective
notification can beat rechecking sources.
